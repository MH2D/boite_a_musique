How the LCD talks to the Raspberry Pi

A plain-language guide to the words and ideas behind wiring a 16×2 character LCD to your Pi: VCC, GND, SDA, SCL, I²C, logic levels, pull-ups, and contrast. Everything we did, and why.

1. The four wires, in one sentence each

Almost every simple electronic module talks to a computer using some combination of four kinds of wire. Two carry power, two carry data.

NameStands forWhat it does
VCCVoltage at the Common Collector The power-in wire. Delivers the supply voltage (3.3 V or 5 V) that makes the device run. Think of it as the "+" terminal of a battery.
GNDGround The return path for electricity, and the shared 0 V reference. Every voltage is measured relative to GND. The "−" terminal.
SDASerial Data The wire the actual data (characters, commands) travels on, one bit at a time.
SCLSerial Clock A metronome tick that says "read the data wire now." Keeps both sides in step.
Why do you always need at least VCC and GND? Electricity flows in a loop. Current leaves the Pi through VCC, does its work in the display, and must return through GND. One wire alone is a dead end — no loop, no current, no light.

2. Voltage, current — the water analogy

The classic mental model:

  • Voltage (volts, V) = water pressure. How hard the electricity is being pushed. Our display runs on 3.3 V or 5 V pressure.
  • Current (amps, A) = water flow rate. How much is actually moving.
  • Resistance (ohms, Ω) = a narrow pipe. Restricts the flow.

They're linked by Ohm's law: V = I × R (pressure = flow × narrowness). We used this earlier to calculate that a 4.7 kΩ resistor limits a stray current to a harmless ~0.36 mA — narrow pipe, tiny trickle.

3. The GPIO header — and which pins we used

The Pi exposes its electrical connections on a double row of 40 pins called the GPIO header (General-Purpose Input/Output). Each pin has a fixed job — you can't move a data wire to just any pin. Pin 1 is the corner pin nearest the SD-card/power edge; numbering zig-zags (odd row / even row).

4. I²C — how two chips share just two wires

I²C ("eye-squared-see", Inter-Integrated Circuit) is the language spoken over SDA + SCL. Its trick: many devices can share the same two wires. Each device has a unique address. The Pi (the "controller") shouts the address onto the bus; only the matching device answers. Your display answered at address 0x27 — that's what the i2cdetect scan confirmed.

What the "backpack" does

A bare 16×2 LCD needs 16 wires. That's a lot of GPIO pins. The little Keyestudio I²C backpack soldered to yours contains a chip (a PCF8574, an "I/O expander") that converts those 16 parallel wires into just the 2 I²C wires. It's a translator that saves you 12 pins.

5. Logic levels & the 3.3 V vs 5 V question

Digital chips represent 1 and 0 as high and low voltages. "High" means roughly the supply voltage. This is a logic level. The catch: the Pi's data pins are built for 3.3 V logic and are not 5 V tolerant — feeding them 5 V stresses them.

This is the whole tension behind our power choice. The display looks best on 5 V, but its data lines then idle at 5 V — above the Pi's 3.3 V rating. We accepted it because a resistor (next section) limits the actual stress to a harmless trickle, and because your display is powered by the Pi, so they switch on and off together.

6. Pull-up resistors — why the data lines sit "high"

I²C wires don't actively drive themselves high. Instead, a pull-up resistor gently ties each line up to VCC, so it rests at "high (1)" by default; a chip sends a "0" by briefly yanking the line down to ground. (This "release-high, pull-low" design lets many chips share the wire without ever fighting each other.)

7. Contrast (V0) — why the screen was blank then dim

A character LCD has two independent halves: the data side (which character sits in which cell) and the optics (a liquid-crystal layer that's only visible when the right voltage hits a pin called V0). You can have perfect data and a lit backlight yet see nothing, because V0 is wrong.

The ideal V0 isn't a fixed number — it drifts with temperature and viewing angle — so the backpack puts a potentiometer (the blue screw = an adjustable resistor) on V0. You turn it to dial in contrast by eye. And because the pot can only reach voltages within the supply range, 3.3 V couldn't get dark enough — which is exactly why moving to 5 V sharpened the text.

8. Putting it together — our boîte à musique

the full chain

Scan barcode → Pi looks it up → plays the song and sends the person's name over I²C → backpack (PCF8574) translates it to the LCD's 16 pins → HD44780 controller stores the characters → liquid crystal (contrast set by the pot) makes them visible.

The display is a visual extra. The code is written so that if the screen is ever unplugged, missing, or faulty, the music box keeps working exactly as before — the LCD never gets a vote on whether a song plays.
